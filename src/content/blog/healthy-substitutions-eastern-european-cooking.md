This is a reference guide for cooking Eastern European food the lighter way: swaps that keep the character of the cuisine while cutting a lot of the fat and calories. Calorie savings are approximate and depend on your exact ingredients and portions.
Save this page. Return to it whenever you are adapting a family recipe, a Slovak cookbook, or a Ukrainian classic that you love.

## Fats and cooking oils

**Lard → A measured spoon of oil, or an oil spray.** Lard and oil have almost the same calories, about 115–120 kcal per tablespoon, so the saving comes from using less. A light spray of oil in a hot non-stick pan does the job of a spoonful of lard. For dishes that need browning (kotlety, potato pancakes), use 1 tsp of olive oil and finish in the oven.

**Sunflower oil (large pours) → Measured oil.** Saves about 120 kcal for every tablespoon you leave out. Many Eastern European recipes call for "a generous glug" of oil, which often turns out to be 3–4 tablespoons when you measure it. One tablespoon does the same job in most dishes.

**Butter for frying → A little ghee or an oil spray.** Butter's milk solids burn at high heat, while ghee has had them removed, so it tolerates searing better. Ghee is not lower in calories (it is slightly higher per gram), so the saving comes from using a teaspoon instead of a knob. Where butter flavour matters (kasha), use half the usual amount and make up the rest with low-sodium stock.

**Duck or goose fat → Small amount of duck fat + stock.** Saves 100+ kcal per serving. You cannot fully replicate the flavour of duck fat, so do not try to eliminate it: use a fraction (½ tsp) to season, and add stock to the pan for body.

**Deep frying → Oven-baking at 220°C.** Saves a lot of fat per serving. Deruny, krokiety, pampushky, all of them work in a hot oven on a preheated baking tray lined with a light oil spray. Not identical to fried, but close.

**Rendered pork crackling as garnish → Skip or use smoked paprika.** Saves 80–100 kcal. Skwarki appear on soups, salads, and side dishes throughout Poland, Slovakia, and Ukraine. A pinch of smoked paprika stirred into a little olive oil and drizzled over gives a similar smoky note.

## Dairy and cream

**Full-fat sour cream → Low-fat Greek yogurt (in hot dishes).** Saves 60–80 kcal per serving. The critical technique: never add yogurt directly to a simmering pot. Remove from heat, let it cool 2 minutes, then stir in the yogurt mixed with ½ tsp cornstarch. This prevents curdling. In paprikash, borscht, and goulash the difference is hard to tell.

**Full-fat sour cream → Low-fat sour cream (in cold dishes).** Saves 40–50 kcal per serving. In cold dishes, cucumber salad, cold dips, cold beet salad, low-fat sour cream works better than yogurt, which can be too tangy.

**Full-fat tvaroh/quark → Low-fat cottage cheese, strained.** Saves 70–100 kcal per serving. Pour low-fat cottage cheese through a sieve or cheesecloth for 30 minutes. The result is close to tvaroh in consistency and works in syrniki, nalysnyky filling, and desserts.

**Whipping cream in sauces → Evaporated skimmed milk + cornstarch.** Saves several hundred calories per pot of sauce. Mix 1 tbsp cornstarch into 200ml evaporated skimmed milk and stir into the sauce off the heat. It thickens well and gives body without the fat. Works in creamy soups and any sauce where cream is used for body rather than flavour.

**Full-fat kefir → Low-fat kefir.** Saves about 30–60 kcal per glass. The flavour difference is small, particularly in baked goods, marinades and cold drinks.

**Condensed milk → Evaporated skimmed milk + a little sugar or sweetener.** Saves a lot of calories in fillings. Condensed milk is very thick and very sweet; the lighter version is thinner, so use it where a creamy sweetness matters more than a set, fudgy texture.

## Meat and protein

**Fatty pork shoulder → Pork tenderloin.** Saves 80–120 kcal per 100g. Tenderloin is one of the leanest cuts on the pig, about 3–5g fat per 100g versus roughly 15–20g in shoulder. It dries out if overcooked, so keep it just cooked through and let it rest. For slow-cooked dishes like bigos, use lean pork leg instead.

**Duck or goose → Chicken thighs (skin-off).** Saves 100–150 kcal per 100g. Chicken thighs have enough fat to stay juicy through long cooking. They absorb marinades well and work in any recipe that calls for duck or goose in a braise or paprikash.

**Minced pork (fatty) → Turkey mince or lean beef mince.** Saves 60–100 kcal per 100g. For kotlety and holubtsi filling. Turkey mince is very lean, so add a little olive oil and one egg yolk to keep the texture moist. Lean beef mince (5% fat) is a direct swap.

**Whole eggs (yolk-heavy use) → Egg whites + 1 yolk.** Saves about 50 kcal per two eggs replaced. In bindings and batters, replace 2 whole eggs with 3 egg whites + 1 yolk. The structure holds and there is still some richness.

**Fatty smoked sausage as main protein → Lean smoked chicken sausage.** Saves 100–150 kcal per 100g. Kielbasa and its Slovak/Czech equivalents are high in fat by design. Lean smoked chicken sausage gives you the smoky flavour in soups and stews with much less fat. Use it in żurek, kapustnica, and lentil soups.

**Canned sardines in oil → Canned sardines in brine or water.** Saves 40–60 kcal per serving. Used in spreads and salads across Eastern European cuisine. The protein is the same, and once seasoned with lemon, onion and herbs the difference is small.

## Grains and carbohydrates

**White bread → Dark rye bread.** More fibre and a stronger flavour. Whole-grain rye keeps you full longer, so two slices often satisfy where three slices of white bread might not.

**White flour (in dumplings and pancakes) → Half oat flour.** Oat flour is not lower in calories, but it adds a lot of fibre, which makes dumplings and pancakes more filling. Replacing half the white flour in halushky, varenyky dough, or syrniki batter with fine oat flour gives a slightly denser texture. Start with a quarter if you want the change to be less noticeable.

**White rice → Buckwheat (grechka).** Saves about 30–40 kcal per 100g cooked, with more protein and fibre and much more magnesium. Buckwheat is already native to Eastern European cuisine: this is not a foreign health food substitution, it is a return to tradition. Toast it briefly before cooking for a nuttier flavour.

**Egg noodles in soups → Fewer noodles + more vegetables.** Saves 80–120 kcal per bowl. Chicken noodle soup and vegetable broths often use noodles as a filler. Halve the noodles and double the carrots, celery root, and parsley root. The soup is just as filling with fewer calories.

**Mashed potato (butter-heavy) → Mashed potato with stock.** Saves 80–100 kcal per serving. Use warm low-sodium chicken stock instead of butter and whole milk. Add roasted garlic and a teaspoon of olive oil for richness. The texture is lighter and the flavour stays rich.

**White sugar in baking → Less sugar.** In Eastern European sweet dishes, medivnyk, pampushky, apple strudel, sugar is often used heavily. Cutting the stated amount by about a third works in most recipes. Sweeteners such as erythritol can replace part of the sugar, but they are not identical: erythritol is less sweet than sugar and does not brown, so expect a different texture and colour.

## Flavour and seasoning

**Salt-heavy seasoning → Herbs, acid, and umami.** Salt does a lot of the work in traditional Eastern European cooking. The lighter approach is to build flavour instead: fresh dill, parsley, bay leaf, and marjoram are all native to the cuisine and add flavour without sodium. A splash of apple cider vinegar or lemon juice at the end of cooking brightens every soup and stew.

**Full-fat mayonnaise in salads → Low-fat Greek yogurt + Dijon mustard.** Saves 80–90 kcal per tablespoon. Mix low-fat Greek yogurt with a teaspoon of Dijon, a squeeze of lemon, and a pinch of salt. This dressing works in Olivier salad, vinegret, and any mayonnaise-based salad.

**Bread thickening in soups → Pureed vegetables.** Saves 60–80 kcal per serving. Old-school Eastern European soups use bread or flour for body. Blend a cup of the cooked vegetables instead. You get a similar thick consistency with more fibre.

**Sugar in pickle brines → Less sugar.** Naturally fermented pickles and sauerkraut need only vegetables, salt and water, no sugar at all. For quick vinegar pickles, you can cut the sugar substantially or use a little sweetener; the pickles will taste sharper.

**Commercial bouillon cubes → Homemade stock or low-sodium stock.** Bouillon cubes are very high in salt. Making stock from chicken or beef bones takes little active time and gives every soup a better base. Freeze in 500ml portions.

## Do these swaps actually work?

Some swaps are close to invisible and others change a dish a little. The ones to be careful with: low-fat cream cheese in cheesecake is noticeable, oat flour on its own makes varenyky dough too dense, and halving the lard in bigos without adding stock or flavour leaves it flat.

Healthy Eastern European cooking is not about discipline or sacrifice. It is about understanding what actually made the original dishes work, and removing only the parts that do not serve that purpose.

## Frequently asked questions

Most of these swaps are hard to notice in the finished dish. Swapping lard for a small amount of oil is the one you might notice, mainly as a slightly different mouthfeel; a pinch of smoked paprika in the oil helps. Low-fat dairy is hard to tell apart in cooked dishes when the yogurt goes in off the heat.

The biggest calorie saving usually comes from measuring fat: replacing large pours of lard or oil with a measured tablespoon or a light spray easily saves 100–200 kcal per meal. The second biggest is full-fat sour cream to low-fat Greek yogurt, which saves 60+ kcal per serving in every dish that traditionally uses a generous dollop.

For cold dishes, cucumber salad, cold beet salad, any dip, low-fat sour cream works better than yogurt, which can be too sharp when not heated. Keep yogurt for hot dishes and sour cream for cold ones.
