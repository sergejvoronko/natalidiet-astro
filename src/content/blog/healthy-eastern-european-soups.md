If you grew up in Eastern or Central Europe, or have ever had a bowl of beet-red borscht on a cold evening, you already know this cuisine was built on soup. Long before anyone called it 'bone broth', soup was everyday food across the region. And soup happens to be the easiest place to eat well here: these are filling, nourishing meals that stay light without losing flavour.
This guide covers the soups worth knowing from Ukraine, Slovakia, Poland, Hungary and across Central Europe, with a recipe link and nutrition numbers for each, plus how to lighten the traditional versions without wrecking them.

## The Eastern European soup pantry, staple ingredients
Before diving into individual recipes, let us look at the building blocks that make these soups so nutritious. If you keep these in your kitchen, you can make almost any Eastern European soup on a weekday:

### Vegetables
- Beets, a good source of folate and manganese; their pigments (betalains) give borscht its deep red colour
- Cabbage, high in vitamin C and K and full of fibre; the backbone of many soups
- Carrots, parsnips & celery root, form the 'holy trinity' base of Eastern European broths
- Potatoes, filling, high in potassium and B6; used as a natural thickener
- Sorrel (sour grass), the star of green borshch; lemony, and a good source of vitamin C

### Fermented & cultured foods
- Sauerkraut, adds the characteristic sour note without vinegar (raw sauerkraut has live cultures, but they do not survive simmering)
- Kefir, used in some lighter soups instead of sour cream; more protein, fewer calories
- Pickle brine, the sour base of rassolnik; kvass (fermented rye bread drink) is the base of the cold soup okroshka
- Sour rye starter (zakwas), the traditional base of Polish zurek

### Protein sources
- Lean beef or chicken for broth, choose lean cuts or skim fat after cooking
- Beans and lentils, traditional in lean-day soups; high protein, high fibre
- Mushrooms, dried porcini and forest mushrooms give deep umami flavour; very low calorie
- Eggs, hard-boiled, added to finished soups for protein

## Healthy swaps that work in every Eastern European soup
Traditional Eastern European recipes often call for lard, fatty pork cuts, or heavy sour cream. These small swaps keep most of the flavour while cutting a lot of calories:
- A teaspoon of olive oil instead of a spoonful of lard to soften the vegetables
- Lean meat, or skimming the fat off the broth once it has cooled
- Low-fat Greek yogurt or light sour cream instead of full-fat sour cream, added off the heat
- More vegetables and pulses in place of some of the meat or noodles

## How to meal-prep Eastern European soups for the week
One handy thing about these soups: they keep well and often taste better the next day as the flavours deepen. Here is a simple weekly routine:
- Sunday: Make a large batch of base vegetable broth (2–3 litres). This is the foundation of everything.
- Split the broth into two pots. Make borscht in one and a lighter vegetable soup in the other.
- Portion into glass containers (400–500ml each). Label with date and calorie count.
- Refrigerate up to 3–4 days or freeze up to 3 months.
- Reheat gently; long, hard boiling dulls the colour of borscht.

## Eastern European soups and weight loss: what the science says
Starting a meal with a low-calorie, broth-based soup can help you eat less overall: in a 2007 study published in the journal *Appetite*, people who began lunch with soup ate about 20% fewer calories at that meal in total, soup included. Eastern European soups make a good first course or a complete light meal because:
- Lots of water and vegetable fibre fill you up for few calories
- Adding beans, lentils, eggs or lean meat brings protein, the most filling nutrient
- They are made from simple, whole ingredients with little processing
- Sour flavours from sauerkraut, pickles or rye starter give big flavour without fat

## Frequently asked questions

### Are Eastern European soups healthy?

Yes, especially in lighter versions. Most are built on vegetables, broth and sour fermented ingredients, so they are low in calories and a good source of fibre and vitamins. The key is making simple modern swaps: replace lard with olive oil, use low-fat dairy, and reduce added salt.

### Can I eat borscht and other Eastern European soups on a weight loss diet?

Absolutely. The soups in this series run about 130–230 calories per generous bowl, which suits calorie-controlled eating. They are also very filling due to their high water and fibre content, so you are less likely to snack afterwards.

### Are Eastern European soups gluten-free?

Most traditional vegetable soups, borscht, green borshch, mushroom soup, are naturally gluten-free. Polish żurek is the notable exception as it is based on fermented rye flour. Rassolnik can be made gluten-free by swapping the barley for rice. Always check stock cubes and sausages for hidden gluten.

### What is the difference between borscht and borshch?

Both spellings refer to the same beet-based soup, just transliterated differently from Cyrillic. 'Borscht' is the Yiddish-influenced English spelling. 'Borshch' is a more direct Ukrainian transliteration. This blog uses both spellings for the same soup.

### How do I make Eastern European soups vegan?

Most of these soups adapt easily to vegan versions. Replace meat broth with mushroom or vegetable broth (dried porcini mushrooms create incredible depth). Skip meat or replace with beans or tofu. Use coconut yogurt or cashew cream instead of sour cream.
